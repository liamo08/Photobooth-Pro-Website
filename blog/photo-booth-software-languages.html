Photo Booth Software in 11 Languages: Filipino, Hindi, Spanish & More

September 29, 2026 8 min read

A photo booth is a self-serve machine. Guests walk up, read the screen, and work it out on their own. If they can't read the screen, they don't use the booth, or they queue behind the one person who can and slow the whole line down. Wedding guests flown in from Manila, a sales team from Jakarta, grandparents at a Punjabi wedding in Dublin: the booth should talk to them in their own language.

That's why BoothLedger ships fully translated into 11 languages. This guide lists every one, explains where each fits, and shows how to switch the booth before your next event.

What gets translated

It isn't just a translated "Touch to start" button. BoothLedger translates the whole app:

  • Guest screens: the start screen, countdown, photo review, retake prompts, sharing by QR code, email and SMS, and print confirmations.
  • Data capture and consent: the built-in wording on data capture forms is translated into every language, so a consent line reads properly to the guest ticking the box.
  • Operator screens: settings, the template designer and the built-in tutorials. That matters if you hire local attendants who aren't confident in English.

Brand names and a few technical terms (camera models, printer names, file formats) deliberately stay in English so they match what's printed on the hardware and in camera menus.

Your own text is yours: the event name, template headlines and custom messages are whatever you type, so you can write them in any language, including ones outside this list.

How to change the photo booth language

1

Pick a language on first launch

The first time BoothLedger opens, it shows a language picker with every language written in its own script. Tap the one your guests speak.

2

Change it any time in Settings

Open Settings → Language and choose any of the 11 languages. Guest screens use it from the next session, so you can run a Spanish wedding on Saturday and a Polish corporate event on Monday on the same booth.

3

Match your template text

Write the event name and headlines on your print template in the same language. For Hindi, pick a font that supports Devanagari so the characters render properly on the print.

4

Run a test session

Do one full session before doors open: start screen, countdown, review, share and print. It takes two minutes and catches any custom text you forgot to translate.

Tips for running a booth in another language

  • Ask the client which language most guests read best. At mixed events, pick the language of the older guests. Younger guests usually cope with English; grandparents often don't.
  • Translate your props and signage too. A "Scan to get your photo" sign in the guests' language gets more shares.
  • Get a native speaker to check custom text. The app wording is fully translated, but your headline and hashtag are your own. A quick check avoids an embarrassing print.
  • Consider your attendant. Operator screens are translated too, so a local attendant can manage the booth in their own language.
